Your image. Their content?

You have an image to use for your employer. What can you publish, edit or share, and who gets to decide?

You took an image for your employer.

Think about your own business, team or department. You took a mobile photo as part of your job and might edit it in Adobe Express for your employer’s website, newsletter or social channels.

Who can approve its use? What changes if a colleague wants to use it in another campaign, or someone outside the business copies it?

Sections 1 and 2 focus on your workplace and your own photos. In Section 3, you will compare image licences, attribution and permitted uses.

Make the call.

Keep your own workplace and photo in mind. For each fictional situation, use the stated facts to choose what you would do, then check the evidence. Discuss your reasoning or add an optional note. Allow about 10–12 minutes.

Read the licence. Make the use work.

Four quick challenges: what can you share, where can you use it, and what has to happen first? Choose an action, then compare the evidence. Allow 4–5 minutes. No photo selection or editing is needed.

Copyright is the right; a licence gives permission. BY, SA, ND and NC are licence conditions, not different forms of copyright. There is no percentage of editing that automatically clears someone else’s image.

CC BY and CC BY-SA allow both unchanged reuse and adaptations, with conditions. They do not require you to change the image first. A bespoke permission could authorise only a particular adapted use; that would depend on its wording.

Keep this licence guide beside you

Open a label when you need it. These examples use CC 4.0; check the exact version and all conditions on a real source. CC0 is a separate public-domain tool.

Stock libraries such as Pexels and Adobe Stock use their own terms. “Royalty-free” does not mean copyright-free. Check channels, paid promotion, edits, duration, territory and account permissions.

Optional: apply the credit and publishing checks

Use the fictional CC BY “Desk light” source record in challenge 4. Add the credit, intended newsletter use and edit conditions in your own words. Caption and alt-text notes can record what you would need to know before publication; there is no actual image to describe here. Notes retained from an earlier version should be reviewed against this new example.

Use alt="" for a genuinely decorative web image. Reassess for an informative social post. A caption gives context, a credit identifies the creator, and alt text conveys relevant visual information.

Keep creator, copyright owner, credit and permitted-use metadata with the source image. Check what survives export and upload. These fields are planning notes; they do not embed metadata or verify permission.

When an exception is suggested: UK exceptions such as quotation, criticism/review, parody or pastiche have specific conditions, including fair dealing where applicable. A new layout, an AI edit or a different message is not automatically an exception. Confirm the basis with the relevant rights colleague.

Your next shoot

  1. Agree the purpose and channels before taking the photo.
  2. Check ownership, participants, location and confidential details.
  3. Use the approved Adobe Express account and check any added assets.
  4. Review whether edits or AI change the meaning.
  5. Write the caption, agreed credit and appropriate alt text.
  6. Keep permissions and rights records. Check the actual export and published post.

Debrief: If someone used your image in a way you disliked, what evidence would you need? Are you giving other creators the same consideration?

Sources and scope

UK workplace learning prototype. Fictional decision situations and licence examples, not Hearst policy or a statement of individual employment terms. Check your organisation’s approvals and the live terms for the account, feature and channel being used. Sources checked 4 November 2026; platform terms and features can change.

    Facilitator: suggested use

    Introduce it as a judgement challenge: “You took this image for your employer. Now decide what can happen to it.” Allow about 1 minute for the workplace context, 10–12 minutes for seven decisions, 4–5 minutes for four licence challenges, and 1–2 minutes for the takeaway. Sections 1 and 2 use the learner’s workplace context and optional own photo. Section 3 compares licence conditions using fictional source records; optional publishing notes can follow. Taking or editing a new photo can happen elsewhere in the session. Learners may work independently or compare reasoning in pairs.

    Ask creators and approvers to exchange perspectives. Invite one changed decision and one unresolved workplace check. Optional notes and the final action are self-reviewed, not automatically marked. Completion means attempting the decisions; it does not certify legal clearance.
